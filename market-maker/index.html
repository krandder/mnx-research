01

The business, in one paragraph

What the market maker is

The market maker is the software that provides liquidity on MNX markets. On every market it runs, it posts a bid (a price at which it will buy) and an ask (a price at which it will sell), and it keeps those quotes live and current. When a user trades, they trade against the maker. The maker earns the spread — the gap between its buy and sell prices — and in exchange it takes on inventory: a position it now has to manage.

At launch the maker may be most of the order book. That single fact drives the whole design. It cannot read prices off its own quotes, and it cannot dump a position by selling into a market that is mostly itself. So the posture is deliberately defensive: quote around an external estimate of fair value, take on risk slowly, charge more when flow looks informed, and stop cleanly when something is wrong. It is far easier to tighten spreads after watching real flow than to recover from being picked off in week one.

Earn the spread

Quote both sides continuously. The difference between bid and ask is the maker's compensation for standing ready to trade.

Manage inventory

Every fill leaves a position. The maker steers its quotes to keep that position, and the vault's risk, inside limits it chooses.

Fail closed

On any doubt — stale data, thin margin, a lost lease — it stops adding risk rather than guessing. Safety beats liveness.

The one design idea

The system is split into two independent layers. A pricing model decides where the maker wants to buy and sell. A quoting engine decides how to place, refresh, and cancel the actual orders that express it. Keeping these apart is what keeps the strategy small enough to review by eye. In the code the split is physical: pure strategy in packages/mm-core/, everything else — exchange calls, database, scheduling — in src/.

Where this lives

  • packages/mm-core/The pure strategy: pricing, ladder, cancel/place decisions. No I/O. The high-review surface.
  • src/The plumbing that runs the strategy against a real venue: clients, control plane, scheduling, telemetry.

02

The pricing model

How it decides where to quote

The center of the maker's quotes is the reservation price. It starts from an external fair price and then shifts for two reasons: how much inventory the maker already holds, and how aggressively users have been trading against it lately. In plain terms:

reservation_price = fair_price × ( 1 − inventory_skew − flow_skew )

All signs are defined from the maker's point of view. A user buy is a maker sell, which lowers maker inventory. Get this convention wrong and the whole model inverts — so it is written into the code and the tests.

Fair price — from outside, never from its own book

The anchor is an external fair-value estimate, not the maker's own displayed mid. If the maker is the book, reading the book just reflects its own quotes back at it — a self-referential loop. If that external price is missing or stale, the maker refuses to quote (see Chapter 5).

Inventory skew — a treasury knob

The more the maker already holds, the less eager it should be to hold more. Long inventory pushes the reservation price down (cheaper to sell, dearer to buy); short inventory pushes it up. This is linear and slow. Its steepness is a business decision about risk appetite — roughly a 1% shift per $100k of inventory in the starting calibration. That is the CEO's dial: how much exposure is the vault willing to warehouse, and at what discount.

Flow skew — the toxicity term

This is the idea that matters most. Recent trades carry more information than old inventory. If users suddenly buy hard right now, that burst is itself evidence someone knows something — so the maker raises its price and sells only at a premium. This term decays exponentially over minutes, and it is calibrated ~5× steeper than inventory (about a 1% shift per $20k of immediate flow). It can override the inventory sign: the maker may be long yet still quote to sell above fair value, because the flow hitting it looks informed.

Spread and volatility

Around the reservation price sits the spread: a base amount, widened when volatility rises. Volatility only widens the spread — in this version it never moves the reservation price and never resizes orders. That rule exists on purpose: computing volatility from the same flow you are reacting to invites manipulation and loss-making feedback loops.

Inventory term asks

“How much balance-sheet exposure do we want to carry?” Slow, linear, a treasury dial.

Flow term asks

“How much might the trader hitting us right now know that we don't?” Fast, decaying, a defense against adverse selection.

  • packages/mm-core/src/quote.tsbuildQuoteModel — reservation price, spread, inventory & flow skew.
  • packages/mm-core/src/flow.tscomputeDecayedRecentFlowUsd — the exponentially decayed recent-flow measure.
  • packages/mm-core/src/regime.tsresolveFairPrice — sources the external fair price and gates on staleness.
03

Depth & scale

The ladder, and adding capital

The maker does not post one bid and one ask. It posts a ladder: around ten orders per side, stepped out on a coarse price grid, so the book looks natural and price impact is gradual rather than a single cliff. Orders live in persistent ring slots — a fixed pool of ~100 slots per side — so the maker updates only the levels that changed instead of cancelling and rebuilding the whole book every cycle. Each slot carries its own lifecycle, which is what makes fills-during-cancel and partial fills safe to reason about.

Add capital, get a deeper book — same shape

The dollar knobs — inventory depth, flow depth, per-level size, maximum inventory — are not hard-coded. They scale with the live equity in the maker's vault. Fund the vault with more, and every level of the ladder grows proportionally: the book gets deeper while its shape stays fixed. Pull equity out, and it shrinks the same way. A slew clamp bounds how fast the scale can move tick-over-tick, so one corrupted balance read cannot inflate sizing, caps, and budget all at once.

Why a shared budget

One process runs every market against one vault. Before it plans, it computes a single ladder budget — total margin minus what is used, with one safety floor held back — and then plans markets in a stable order, charging each ladder's opening margin against the remainder. Markets draw from a common pool of capital, fairly and predictably.

Where this lives

  • packages/mm-core/src/ladder.tsbuildLadder — the margin-bounded, ring-slot ladder.
  • packages/mm-core/src/slots.tsselectActiveRingSlots — ring-slot selection and sizing generation.
  • packages/mm-core/src/sizing.tsbuildOrderForLevel — per-level size and side capacities.
  • src/capital-scaling.tsresolveCapitalScaledMarkets and applyEquitySlew — equity → knobs, with the slew clamp.
04

A guided tour

One tick, end to end

Everything above runs on a tick: a single quoting cycle fired on a cadence and on wake-on-move interrupts. A tick reads the operator's intent, plans every market, executes in a safety-ordered sequence, and reports what it did. It touches exactly eight files, and each hop names the next — no repo-wide search needed to follow it. This is also the recommended reading path.

1
src/control-plane.ts

Read the operator's row from Postgres — process mode, vault, capital fractions, per-market config. No control row for this environment means the service runs disabled: an empty control DB can never start quoting.

2
src/client.ts

The MNX venue adapter merges those rows with live exchange state — balances, positions, open orders, fills — into the planner's inputs, and owns order placement and cancellation.

3
src/runtime.ts — runTick

The linear audit trail. Renew the single-instance lease once, load state, then resolve the effective mode through the most-restrictive-wins lattice before anything trades.

4
src/tick-plan.ts

The planning fold: apply capital scaling and the equity slew, derive the shared-vault budget, then plan markets one at a time against the running budget remainder.

5
packages/mm-core/src/planner.ts mm-core · pure

The strategy core. Given a snapshot, config, fair price, risk, fills and open orders, it returns a plan — regime, desired orders, what to cancel, what to place — with no I/O at all. This is Chapters 2 and 3, in code.

6
src/tick-pipeline.ts

Execution, in a fixed, fail-closed order: cancels first for every market, then one aggregate validation gate, then placements — isolated per market. Reducing exposure always runs before adding any.

7
src/telemetry-snapshot.ts

Turn the tick's result into the dashboard wire shape: per-market quotes, orders, positions, signals, and health.

8
src/telemetry-publisher.ts

POST the snapshot to the operator dashboard. Publish failures are swallowed with a warning — telemetry can never break the trading loop. The tick is now observable at mm.mnx.fi; the next tick starts again at hop 1.

05

The section that earns trust

How it stays safe — it fails closed

A market-maker bug spends capital fast, so the system is built around one rule: on uncertainty, refuse new exposure. On error, contention, or missing configuration, the maker cancels or holds rather than proceeding. And when it stops, it holds its position rather than liquidating — dumping inventory into a market that is mostly itself only deepens the loss.

There are 25 catalogued fail-closed mechanisms, each pinned by golden snapshot tests so its behavior cannot drift silently. They fall into a few layers:

Group A

Strategy regime gates

Per market, per tick, the first tripped gate wins: a missing or stale fair price, margin below the floor, exhausted hit-points, an unconfigured grid, or a failed liquidity-impact invariant all drop the market to cancel-only; inventory above its cap drops it to reduce-only. packages/mm-core/src/regime.ts

Group B

Lease & the mode lattice

Exactly one process may quote, held by an atomic single-instance lease. The effective mode is the most restrictive of the environment mode, the operator's row, and whether the lease is held — disabled > cancel_only > quote. Lose the lease and the tick caps at cancel-only. src/lease.ts · src/tick-pipeline.ts

Group C

Execution ordering

Cancels run before any other failable step; a per-market error skips that market's placements without touching the others; an aggregate validation gate can fail the whole tick after the risk-reducing cancels have already run; and the lease is re-checked one last time immediately before each market places. src/tick-pipeline.ts

Group D

Watchdogs & independent backstop

A venue-side dead-man's switch cancels every order if the process goes quiet, and it is re-armed only at the end of healthy ticks — so a stuck-but-alive process still lets the cancel-all fire. A separate backstop service independently closes stale positions when the maker is not doing its job. src/dead-man-switch.ts · src/backstop-runtime.ts

Startup

Continuity guards

A large away-from-market jump in the fair price holds the market's opening quotes until a human confirms — reduce-only closes still pass. Boot without a control database fails fast rather than trading degraded. src/tick-plan.ts · src/bootstrap.ts

Two things fail open, on purpose

Heartbeat writes and the tick loop itself never crash the process. The reasoning is deliberate: a dead process strands live orders on the venue with nothing managing them — strictly worse than a running but degraded process that keeps cancelling and reporting. The dead-man's switch and the backstop cover the wedged-process case from the outside.

06

The map

Architecture & where to read next

The architecture is one boundary drawn firmly. packages/mm-core/ is pure: it holds the quote math, inventory skew, ladder sizing, cancel/place decisions, and regime logic, and it may not import HTTP clients, databases, timers, or anything with a side effect. Everything a trade decision depends on is a function of its inputs, which is why the same plan can be replayed and snapshot-tested exactly. src/ is plumbing: it fetches the inputs, executes the outputs, and holds the mutable state the core is forbidden to keep. When you read the code, that line tells you what a file is allowed to do.

Two reference docs are the map. The concept → file map answers “where does X live?” — every concept points to exactly one owning file. The tick walkthrough is the narrative path. Start with the eight files below, in order; it is the same path Chapter 4 walked.

  1. src/control-plane.tsthe operator's row, read from Postgres

  7. src/telemetry-snapshot.tstick result → dashboard wire shape
