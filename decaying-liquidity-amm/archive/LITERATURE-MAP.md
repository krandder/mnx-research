# Literature map: the information model behind the decaying-liquidity AMM

Companion to RESEARCH-BRIEF-2.md. Every citation below was checked against the journal page, a RePEc/SSRN/arXiv/HAL record, or the author PDF on 2026-09-22, and every DOI was resolved. Each entry ends with a verification note: "Verified" means the citation and the substantive claims were read from the source (full text where open: Alfonsi–Schied 2010, Gatheral's slides, Guéant 2013, Banerjee–Green draft, Back–Crotty–Li draft, Bagnoli et al. author copy, Collin-Dufresne–Fos draft) or from the publisher abstract; "not fully verified" or "not read" flags a claim about a paper's interior (a theorem condition, a shape result) reconstructed from citing papers or standard expositions because the publisher blocked the text. Features referenced: (i) decaying alarm φ, (ii) steepening T, (iii) permanent updates S′(P) + λ|T(φ)|, and the no-loop property.

## 1. Execution and price-manipulation side (mechanics of T, φ-decay, S, and loops)

### Huberman, G. and Stanzl, W. (2004). "Price Manipulation and Quasi-Arbitrage." *Econometrica* 72(4), 1247–1275.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00531.x (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v72y2004i4p1247-1275.html)

Primitives: discrete trades q_n; each trade shifts the price permanently by U(q_n) (a function of that trade's size alone, added across trades, time-independent) and its own execution price by a temporary term; prices are uncertain when orders are placed. A "quasi-arbitrage" is a sequence of round trips with unbounded expected profit and unbounded Sharpe ratio. Theorem: with time-independent permanent impact, "only linear price-impact functions rule out quasi-arbitrage"; with a temporary component added, "only the permanent price impact must be linear while the temporary one can be of a more general form." Relation to us: this is the reason a *nonlinear, additive-in-trade-size* permanent term is unsafe; our S′(P)·dq is not of that form (it depends on the cumulative position P, so ∫S′(P)dP is a potential and every round trip returns it to its start), and HS's theorem does not cover it (see Guéant 2013 below). Kyle's linear λ is the one additive case HS allow, and it is our S′ = const special case. Verified from the RePEc abstract.

### Gatheral, J. (2010). "No-dynamic-arbitrage and market impact." *Quantitative Finance* 10(7), 749–759.
https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680903373692 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=1292353 ; slides (same results) https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/3683/ecb4dae470f4df219d65bb6491d3c7fd4b78.pdf

Primitives: S_t = S_0 + ∫₀ᵗ f(ẋ_s) G(t−s) ds + noise; f is the instantaneous impact of the trading *rate* and G is a decay kernel acting on the *price displacement*. Price manipulation = a round trip with negative expected cost. Results read from the text: (a) permanent impact (G ≡ 1) must be odd and, from the small-ρ expansion, linear ("Non-linear permanent market impact is inconsistent with the principle of no-dynamic-arbitrage"); (b) Lemma: "If temporary market impact decays exponentially, price manipulation is possible unless f(v) ∝ v" (the explicit counterexample is f = √v, v₁ = 0.2, v₂ = 1, ρ = 1, cost −0.0017: accumulate slowly, liquidate fast); (c) power-law kernel G(τ) = τ^{−γ} and f(v) ∝ v^δ: no manipulation at small sizes *requires* γ + δ ≥ 1 (a necessary condition, not sufficient: Curato, Gatheral and Lillo 2017, arXiv:1412.4839, exhibit manipulation at γ = 0.45, δ = 0.55 with enough trades, and a second necessary bound γ ≥ 2 − log₂3 ≈ 0.415 appears in the paper's appendix); with γ = 1 any f ≥ 0 is admissible; (d) he also notes "market impact must be convex for very large v" (finite book). Relation to us: (b) looks like it kills our steepening T with exponential decay, but Gatheral's decay is of the *price* displacement produced by a rate-impact function, whereas our φ decays in *volume* and the price is T(φ); Alfonsi–Schied 2010 show those two models have opposite manipulation properties, and ours is the safe one. (a) is HS's linearity again; it constrains additive permanent impact, not our potential S(P). Verified from the author's slides text (Ecole Polytechnique, 5 Jan 2009), which state the same lemmas as the QF paper.

### Obizhaeva, A. A. and Wang, J. (2013). "Optimal trading strategy and supply/demand dynamics." *Journal of Financial Markets* 16(1), 1–32.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2012.09.001 (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v16y2013i1p1-32.html)

Primitives: a block-shaped limit order book of constant density q, a large trader's market orders eat the block, and the displacement recovers exponentially at resilience rate ρ toward the fundamental; impact is linear (1/q per share) with a permanent component λ. Because the book is a block, volume resilience and price resilience coincide (D = E/q). Result: optimal execution is two discrete blocks at start and end plus a constant rate in between; the cost depends on ρ, not on static depth. Relation to us: the decay of φ with τ = 1/ρ, the exact "retrace on the way back" accounting, and the transient/permanent split are OW's, with T linear; steepening T and any information content are absent (no informed trader; resilience is a mechanical primitive). Verified from the RePEc abstract.

### Alfonsi, A., Fruth, A. and Schied, A. (2010). "Optimal execution strategies in limit order books with general shape functions." *Quantitative Finance* 10(2), 143–157.
https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680802595700 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/0708.1756

Primitives: OW's model with the block replaced by a general density f(x) of limit orders at price offset x (the "shape function"), so a market order of size ξ walks the book and impact is nonlinear; two resilience mechanisms, "the exponential recovery of the number of limit orders" (volume impact, our φ) or "the exponential recovery of the bid-ask spread" (price impact). Result: Theorem 4.1 (volume model): if h₁(y) = F⁻¹(y) − e^{−ρτ}F⁻¹(e^{−ρτ}y) is one-to-one the unique optimal strategy is deterministic and buy-only (initial block, N − 1 equal trades that exactly replace the refilled volume, final block); Remark 4.3: this holds "e.g. if f is decreasing for y > 0 and increasing for y < 0"; the price model (Theorem 5.1) needs an extra growth condition, and Example 5.5 gives a decreasing f for which the volume model works and the price model fails. A closed form for OW's block case, and robustness of the strategy to f and to the resilience type. Relation to us: this is where "φ decays in units and the price of the next unit is F⁻¹(φ)" is written down; our T(φ) is their F⁻¹, our density 1/T′ is their f, and steepening T is exactly the case Remark 4.3 singles out. Manipulation is not the topic here. Verified from the arXiv full text (via the research agent).

### Alfonsi, A. and Schied, A. (2010). "Optimal trade execution and absence of price manipulations in limit order book models." *SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics* 1(1), 490–522.
https://doi.org/10.1137/090762786 ; open text HAL https://hal.science/hal-00397652v3/document ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=1499209

Primitives (read from the HAL text): zero-spread version of AFS; volume impact E_t jumps by the order size and "dE_t = −ρ_t E_t dt" between orders (Model with volume impact reversion), or the price impact D_t reverts instead (Model with price impact reversion); shape function f > 0 a.e.; standing assumption (10): F(x) = ∫₀ˣ f is unbounded in both directions ("the limit order book has infinite depth"). Definitions 2.5–2.6: price manipulation is a round trip with negative expected cost; transaction-triggered price manipulation is when a buy (sell) program is made cheaper by intermediate sells (buys). Assumption 2.9 (volume model): (a) f nondecreasing on ℝ₋ and nonincreasing on ℝ₊, or (b) f(x) = λ|x|^α. Proposition 2.10 and Corollary 2.12: under Assumption 2.9 the optimal strategy exists, is unique, and has all trades of the same sign, hence "the Model with volume impact reversion admits neither standard nor transaction-triggered price manipulation"; the text adds that this "remains valid even for the case in which resilience ρ_t is stochastic." For the price-reversion model a stronger Assumption 2.15 (conditions (20)–(21) on x f′/f and f″, or a power law) is needed (Corollary 2.18). Relation to us: this is our M0 exactly. Assumption 2.9(a) says the density thins away from the touch, i.e. T = F⁻¹ is convex on ℝ₊ (steepening), and (10) says T is unbounded (no saturation): the operator's two shape requirements are precisely the hypotheses under which the theorem holds, and it also covers concave power laws via (b). Note that Corollary 2.12 bundles standard and transaction-triggered manipulation, which is why it needs a condition on f; for *standard* manipulation alone the volume model is safe for every shape function, because the impact cost of a trade is G(E + ξ) − G(E) with G(y) = ∫₀ʸ F⁻¹ convex and G(0) = 0, and exponential shrinkage of E between trades only dissipates potential (G(ay) ≤ aG(y) for a < 1). That is the M4-SAFETY loop identity, not a theorem AS state; cite it as our corollary of their model. Their explanation of the contrast with Gatheral is the volume-versus-price resilience distinction, spelled out in Gatheral, Schied and Slynko (2011), "Exponential resilience and decay of market impact", in Abergel et al. (eds), *Econophysics of Order-driven Markets*, Springer, 225–236, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-88-470-1766-5_15 (abstract only: the two results are "perfectly compatible" because a nonlinear map of a decaying volume state is not a superposition of decaying per-trade price impacts). Verified from the full text.

### Alfonsi, A., Schied, A. and Slynko, A. (2012). "Order Book Resilience, Price Manipulation, and the Positive Portfolio Problem." *SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics* 3(1), 511–533.
https://doi.org/10.1137/110822098 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=1498514 ; Schied's IPAM slides https://helper.ipam.ucla.edu/publications/fin2010/fin2010_8956.pdf

Primitives: linear transient impact with a general decay kernel G (block book), expected cost C(X) = Σ x_i x_j G(|t_i − t_j|) plus linear permanent and instantaneous parts. Results (read from the slides, which reproduce the paper's statements): C(X) ≥ 0 for all strategies iff G(|·|) is the Fourier transform of a positive finite measure (Bochner; strict positivity if the support is not discrete), so exponential decay is strictly positive definite; if G is nonconstant, nonincreasing and convex the optimal strategy is unique and monotone, hence no transaction-triggered manipulation; if G(0) − G(s) < G(t) − G(t+s) for some s, t > 0 (G not convex near 0) transaction-triggered manipulation exists for the grid {0, s, t+s}. Relation to us: our kernel is exponential, so both conditions hold on the linear part of T; the paper is linear in impact and does not address a nonlinear T, which is why AS 2010 is the reference for M0 and this one is the reference for "which decay shapes are admissible" (exponential is the safe canonical case; the operator's τ is unconstrained). Verified from the slides and the SIAM record.

### Gatheral, J., Schied, A. and Slynko, A. (2012). "Transient linear price impact and Fredholm integral equations." *Mathematical Finance* 22(3), 445–474.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9965.2011.00478.x

Continuous-time companion of ASS 2012 (linear transient impact, general kernel): existence and uniqueness of optimal strategies via a Fredholm equation; absence of manipulation iff G is positive definite; existence and monotone strategies for nonincreasing convex G; for some smooth kernels (Gaussian, e^{−ρt²}) no optimal strategy exists in continuous time. Relation to us: same as above; the exponential kernel is in the good class. Citation verified from the journal record; theorem statements from the IPAM slides.

### Guéant, O. (2013/2014). "Permanent market impact can be nonlinear." arXiv:1305.0413 (q-fin.TR), v4 17 March 2014 (unpublished working paper).
https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.0413

Primitives (read from the text): Almgren–Chriss-type continuous trading at rate v_t with permanent impact dS = f(|q₀ − q_t|) v_t dt, i.e. the permanent shift per unit traded depends on the *cumulated* volume executed so far, plus an instantaneous cost h(t, v)v. Proposition 2 (No-dynamic arbitrage): for any absolutely continuous round trip q(0) = q(T), E[X_T] ≤ X₀; the proof writes the permanent part as G(q₀ − q_t) with G(z) = ∫₀ᶻ y f(|y|) dy, a potential, so it vanishes on a round trip. He states explicitly that this is the assumption dropped from HS/Gatheral ("permanent market impact is not only a function of the traded volume but also of the cumulated volume executed so far"). Relation to us: this is our S′(P) charged at the live position, feature (iii)'s floor, and the loop-identity argument in M4-SAFETY.md is the same telescoping; it supports nonlinear S with no manipulation and leaves the displacement-scaled term λ|T(φ)| open (that term is not a potential, which is why it needs the cap). Verified from the full text.

### Kyle, A. S. and Obizhaeva, A. A. (2016). "Market Microstructure Invariance: Empirical Hypotheses." *Econometrica* 84(4), 1345–1404.
https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA10486

Only the impact-shape content: invariance hypotheses (bets arrive in "business time" with an invariant distribution of bet size in risk units) do not by themselves fix a functional form; the authors fit two nested cost specifications on 400,000+ portfolio-transition orders, a bid-ask term plus linear impact and a bid-ask term plus square-root impact, and both fit (they use the linear version, κ̄_I ≈ 2.50, for crash extrapolations; their later "market impact puzzle" work puts the exponent nearer 0.6 and notes a square root is hard to distinguish from linear-plus-spread). So empirically impact versus bet size is concave to at most linear, never convex. Relation to us: this is about the average impact of a metaorder versus its total size, aggregated over decay and over the trader's own scheduling; it is not the marginal price of the next unit as a function of a decayed state. Our T convex in φ is compatible with a concave metaorder curve only if the curve is measured across decay (a patient buyer pays mostly S, and φ stays small); if the operator expects metaorder impact to be measured from the AMM's fills alone, the steepening T predicts convex impact at the burst level, which the empirical square-root literature contradicts. Leaves (i) and (iii) open. Citation verified; specification forms from the working-paper text and the authors' follow-up, not the Econometrica PDF.

### Farmer, J. D., Gerig, A., Lillo, F. and Waelbroeck, H. (2013). "How efficiency shapes market impact." *Quantitative Finance* 13(11), 1743–1758.
https://doi.org/10.1080/14697688.2013.848464 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.5457

Primitives: metaorders ("typically split into small pieces and executed incrementally") whose total volume follows "the commonly observed asymptotic distribution for the volume of large trades" (a power law); a stylized execution service and a fair-pricing condition, the metaorder's average transaction price equals "the price after trading is completed". Result: "the distribution of trading volume adjusts to reflect information, and dictates the shape of the impact function"; impact is concave in metaorder size, asymptotically "roughly as the square root of metaorder size", and permanent impact relaxes on average "to about two thirds of peak impact". The mechanism is that the conditional expected remaining size of a heavy-tailed metaorder grows with the amount already executed, so each further piece is less surprising. Relation to us: this is the sharpest statement that heavy tails in the *size* of informed flow give **concave** impact under fair pricing, whereas Back 1992's heavy tails in the *value* give **convex** impact; feature (ii) must therefore be pinned to the value-jump prior (or to uncertain presence), not to the flow-size prior, and the two effects fight in a model that has both. Verified from the arXiv abstract; the size-distribution exponent is in the body, not read here.

## 2. Information side (where T's shape, the alarm, and the permanent updates come from)

### Kyle, A. S. (1985). "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading." *Econometrica* 53(6), 1315–1335.
https://doi.org/10.2307/1913210 ; https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1985/11/01/continuous-auctions-and-insider-trading

Primitives: one risk-neutral insider who knows v ~ N(p₀, Σ₀); noise trade u ~ N(0, σ_u²) independent of v; competitive risk-neutral market makers see only total flow y = x + u and set p = E[v | y]. Single auction: x = β(v − p₀), p = p₀ + λy with λ = ½√Σ₀/σ_u; in the continuous-time limit "prices follow Brownian motion, the depth of the market is constant over time, and all private information is incorporated into prices by the end of trading." The price is linear in *cumulative* flow, impact is entirely permanent, and there is no decaying state. Relation to us: contradicts (i) (the state is P, never φ); contradicts (ii) (Gaussian prior gives a linear response, neither saturating nor steepening); on (iii) it is the pure floor S′ = λ with no displacement dependence, and HS 2004 show it is the only additive permanent rule that is loop-safe. Prices are a martingale in the market maker's filtration, so no uninformed round trip earns in expectation. Also note Kyle's price is set *before* the fill is known to be a buy or a sell (a batch price), which is the distinction the martingale bridge in the brief has to handle. Verified (publisher abstract; formulas from lecture notes).

### Back, K. (1992). "Insider Trading in Continuous Time." *Review of Financial Studies* 5(3), 387–409.
https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/5.3.387 (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v5y1992i3p387-409.html)

Primitives: continuous-time Kyle with a *general* distribution F for v, Brownian noise flow σB, competitive market makers pricing P_t = H(t, Y_t) as a function of cumulative total flow. "There is a unique equilibrium pricing rule within a certain class," "obtained in closed form for general distributions of the asset value"; lognormal v gives geometric Brownian motion; the insider "does not correlate his trades locally with the noise trades nor does he submit discrete orders." Structure (confirmed through Back–Cocquemas–Ekren–Lioui arXiv:2006.09518 and Ekren–Mostowski–Žitković arXiv:2204.11069, which restate the construction): H solves the backward heat equation and its terminal section is the monotone transport of N(0, σ²T) to F, i.e. H(T, y) = F⁻¹(Φ(y/σ√T)); Y is a Brownian motion in the market maker's filtration and P is a martingale. Relation to us: this is the cleanest support for (ii). The terminal response is the quantile function of the value prior composed with the normal CDF, so a bounded prior saturates (Glosten–Milgrom shape), a Gaussian prior is linear (Kyle), tails heavier than Gaussian steepen (lognormal gives exp(cy); Laplace tails give ~y²; power tails faster), and a spike-and-slab prior (mass at "no change") gives a dead zone near y = 0 with follow-through far out, which is the operator's "shrink near zero, follow far out." Impact is permanent and a function of cumulative flow (contradicts (i)); the local slope H_y grows with displacement for convex H, which is the nearest analogue of the |T(φ)|-scaled permanent update without any transient/permanent split. Verified (citation and abstract; the F⁻¹∘Φ form via citing papers).

### Glosten, L. R. and Milgrom, P. R. (1985). "Bid, Ask and Transaction Prices in a Specialist Market with Heterogeneously Informed Traders." *Journal of Financial Economics* 14(1), 71–100.
https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3

Primitives: risk-neutral competitive specialist; traders arrive one at a time and trade one unit; each is informed (signal about V) or a liquidity trader; the specialist quotes ask = E[V | history, next order is a buy] and bid = E[V | history, next order is a sell], i.e. the posterior *including the fact that the current order executes at that quote*. Results: a spread exists purely from adverse selection; transaction prices are a martingale in the specialist's information; specialist and insider beliefs converge as trades accumulate. In the binary case each buy multiplies the odds of V_H by a constant, so the posterior mean is a logistic function of the net buy count: saturating at the bounds. Relation to us: the "including that fill" convention of the brief's martingale bridge is exactly the GM quote; the bounded prior is the saturating benchmark that (ii) rejects; the per-trade update is largest when the posterior is most uncertain and vanishes at the bounds, the opposite of monotone displacement scaling in (iii); no clock-time decay (contradicts (i)); no uninformed round trip earns in expectation. Verified.

### Easley, D. and O'Hara, M. (1987). "Price, Trade Size, and Information in Securities Markets." *Journal of Financial Economics* 19(1), 69–90.
https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(87)90029-8

Primitives: GM-type sequential trade with two trade sizes and *event uncertainty*: an information event occurs with probability α, the signal is low with probability δ, informed traders are present (fraction μ) only if an event occurred and "prefer to trade larger amounts at any given price"; the market maker quotes size-dependent conditional expectations. Results: large trades execute at worse prices; separating (informed trade only large) or pooling equilibria; "both the size and the sequence of trades matter." Relation to us: the posterior probability that an event occurred is an explicit state updated by every trade, which supports the existence of the "episode active" state in (i), but there is no time decay and no Poisson restart; bounded value so the response saturates (against (ii)); for (iii) the impact of a trade grows with the current event posterior until the direction is learned, then saturates, i.e. a hump rather than a monotone increase in displacement. Verified (abstract; parameter names via the PIN literature).

### Easley, D. and O'Hara, M. (1992). "Time and the Process of Security Price Adjustment." *Journal of Finance* 47(2), 577–605.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04402.x (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v47y1992i2p576-605.html)

Primitives: the day is divided into intervals; before the day an event occurs with probability α (bad with δ); in each interval one trader is drawn and, if an event occurred, is informed with probability μ and always trades in the signal's direction; uninformed traders buy, sell or abstain with fixed probabilities; the market maker updates on buy, sell and *no trade* and quotes conditional expectations. "Time affects prices, with the time between trades affecting spreads"; no-trade intervals are correlated with volume; observed variance is overstated and heteroskedastic. Mechanism: P(no trade | event) = (1 − μ)·P(no trade | no event), so each no-trade interval multiplies the posterior odds of an event by (1 − μ), and the quotes revert toward the no-event value when flow stops. Relation to us: this is the information-side origin of decay in (i): the alarm falls in quiet time *because* an active informed trader would have traded, at a rate set by the informed intensity μ, so τ ≈ 1/μ in interval units; the decay is geometric in the number of no-trade intervals (exponential in clock time only because intervals have fixed length) and acts on the *odds*, so for a small alarm the probability itself decays exponentially. The sufficient statistic is the count vector (buys, sells, no-trades), not one net-flow number, and there is one event per day with no restart; the value is binary so the response in the net count is logistic (saturating for large counts, but convex, ≈ exponential in the log-odds, while the alarm is small). It separates a transient component (reversion during quiet periods) from a permanent one (the realized value), as (iii) does. Verified (citation, abstract; the (1 − μ) factor reconstructed from the standard exposition and a citing paper, not read from the article).

### Holden, C. W. and Subrahmanyam, A. (1992). "Long-Lived Private Information and Imperfect Competition." *Journal of Finance* 47(1), 247–270.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb03985.x

Primitives: multi-auction Kyle with M ≥ 2 risk-neutral insiders who observe the *same* long-lived signal about a normal value, normal noise each auction, linear pricing p_n = p_{n−1} + λ_n·(flow). Result: "such traders compete aggressively and cause most of their common private information to be revealed very rapidly. In the limit, as the interval between auctions approaches zero, market depth becomes infinite and all private information is revealed immediately." Relation to us: this is the primitive behind "competition forces urgency" in (i): informed flow is front-loaded right after information arrives and then stops, so an informed episode is a short burst; the response is linear and permanent (against (ii)); λ_n follows a deterministic time profile within the episode (highest at the start), a time-dependence rather than a displacement-dependence for (iii). Verified (abstract).

### Glosten, L. R. (1994). "Is the Electronic Open Limit Order Book Inevitable?" *Journal of Finance* 49(4), 1127–1161.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb02450.x

Primitives: competitive risk-neutral limit-order suppliers post a schedule; an arriving trader with a private valuation walks the book until the marginal limit price meets his marginal valuation, so the total market order Q is endogenous. Zero expected profit on each marginal unit gives the *tail expectation* rule: the price of the q-th unit is h(q) = E[V | Q ≥ q] on the ask side (E[V | Q ≤ q] on the bid side), which is the "price for the next infinitesimal unit" of our R = F + T(φ) in a one-shot setting; the abstract's results are a small-trade positive spread, maximal liquidity in extreme states, and immunity to third-market competition. Shape: h is nondecreasing, lies weakly above the point-conditional E[V | Q = q], is bounded by sup V (saturates for bounded V), and steepens for heavy-tailed V. Relation to us: supports pricing the marginal unit at a conditional expectation that includes the current fill and everything beyond it, and gives the same verdict as Back on (ii) (tails of the value prior decide steepening versus saturation); static, so (i), (iii) and dynamic loops are left open. Verified (abstract; tail-expectation rule via two citing papers).

### Rochet, J.-C. and Vila, J.-L. (1994). "Insider Trading without Normality." *Review of Economic Studies* 61(1), 131–152.
https://doi.org/10.2307/2297880 (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v61y1994i1p131-152..html)

Primitives: single-auction Kyle in which the insider observes the noise order u before choosing x (equivalently Kyle 1989 with one risk-neutral insider and competitive risk-neutral market makers); the value distribution is arbitrary; the market maker prices p(y) = E[v | y]. Results: a unique equilibrium exists "independently of the distribution of uncertainty," and it "minimizes the expected gains of the informed agent under incentive compatibility constraints" (a variational characterization); the pricing rule is nonlinear whenever the distributions are not the matched pair that makes E[v | y] linear. Relation to us: supports (ii) at the level of existence: a competitive posterior-mean price with a non-Gaussian value prior is a well-defined nonlinear schedule, and the schedule's shape is inherited from the prior; the paper does not characterize convexity, is static (no (i)), and has purely permanent impact. Verified (abstract).

### Bagnoli, M., Viswanathan, S. and Holden, C. W. (2001). "On the Existence of Linear Equilibria in Models of Market Making." *Mathematical Finance* 11(1), 1–31.
https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9965.00106

Primitives: Kyle-type batch models, GM/Easley–O'Hara one-at-a-time models, and call-market models, with N symmetrically or differentially informed strategic traders, independent noise u, and competitive market makers pricing E[v | flow]. Result (Theorem 3.1, read from the author copy https://host.kelley.iu.edu/cholden/Bagnoli-Viswanathan-Holden%20(2001).pdf): a linear equilibrium exists iff the demeaned insider order is an N-fold average of the demeaned noise; for one insider, β(s − μ) must have the same distribution as u (characteristic-function condition), and then the linear equilibrium is unique with λ = ½√(σ_s²/σ_u²) (Theorem 3.3). Table 3.1 lists the admissible pairs (normal, gamma, chi-squared, inverse Gaussian, binomial, negative binomial for any N; Laplace, logistic, uniform, triangular, Poisson, geometric only for N = 1); linearity for every N forces stable laws (Theorems 3.4–3.5); the nonlinear case is not characterized. Relation to us: the contrapositive is the useful part for (ii): the value's shape must match the noise's shape for linearity, so a value prior with heavier tails than the noise is exactly the exit from Kyle's line, and steepening T is the generic case rather than an exotic one; silent on the direction of curvature, on decay, and on manipulation beyond the martingale property. Verified.

### Cho, K.-H. and El Karoui, N. (2000). "Insider Trading and Nonlinear Equilibria: Single Auction Case." *Annales d'Économie et de Statistique* 60, 21–41.
https://www.jstor.org/stable/20076254 (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/adr/anecst/y2000i60p21-41.html)

Primitives: single-auction Kyle with no restriction on the equilibrium's form or on the value distribution; equilibrium is "a fixed point of an operator, which depends only on the distribution of the asset value," with a necessary and sufficient existence condition. Results: for a Bernoulli (good/bad news) value there is a unique equilibrium "in which the price is strongly nonlinear and has plausible empirical counterparts" (the bounded case, hence saturating in the tails); for continuous distributions a class of explicit equilibria is characterized, with Kyle's linear one as a special case; a risk-averse insider gives a unique linear equilibrium with lower price pressure. Relation to us: same verdict as Back 1992 for (ii), in one period: shape follows the value prior, binary saturates; nothing on (i) or decay; permanent impact only. Verified (abstract).

### Collin-Dufresne, P. and Fos, V. (2016). "Insider Trading, Stochastic Liquidity, and Equilibrium Prices." *Econometrica* 84(4), 1441–1475.
https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA10789

Primitives: continuous-time Kyle in which noise-trading volatility "follows a general stochastic process" observed by the insider (and, in the main case, by the market maker). Results: "price impact and price volatility are both stochastic," driven by uninformed-volume shocks "even though the fundamental value is constant"; insiders "trade more aggressively (and thus more information is revealed) when uninformed volume is higher and price impact is lower"; the price is still linear in cumulative flow with a state-dependent λ_t, so the response is linear at each instant. Relation to us: this is the reference for a state-dependent Kyle λ, our "|φ| as an adverse-selection estimate" in (iii): the permanent update per unit is a function of a liquidity state rather than a constant; but their state is exogenous noise volatility, not an endogenous alarm derived from flow, and λ falls when volume rises, whereas our λ|T(φ)| rises with one-directional flow, so the two mechanisms move in opposite directions for balanced volume and agree only for directional volume. Nothing on (ii) curvature or on decay. Verified (abstract).

### Back, K. and Baruch, S. (2004). "Information in Securities Markets: Kyle Meets Glosten and Milgrom." *Econometrica* 72(2), 433–465.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00497.x ; https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2004/03/01/information-securities-markets-kyle-meets-glosten-and-milgrom

Primitives: continuous-time Glosten–Milgrom; one risk-neutral informed trader who knows the binary value v ∈ {0, 1} and is present with certainty; uninformed buys and sells of fixed size δ arrive as independent Poisson processes at rate β per side; the game ends at an exponential announcement time; competitive market makers see each trade and post ask a(p) = E[v | buy] and bid b(p) = E[v | sell] as functions of the current posterior p = Pr(v = 1). The informed trader's equilibrium strategy is a point process with an intensity that depends only on p ("a mixed strategy between trading and waiting"), and "in some circumstances, may randomize over trades that go against his information" (bluffing); as δ → 0 with β scaled, the equilibrium converges to the continuous-time Kyle equilibrium. Price impact of a buy, (a_δ(p) − p)/δ, is hump-shaped in p and vanishes near p ∈ {0, 1}: in cumulative-flow terms a saturating logistic curve. Relation to us: this is the exact primitive set the brief proposes for the special case (Poisson noise per side, quotes that include the fill, an informed intensity that depends on the mispricing, and time between trades carrying information because the informed intensity depends on p), and it is the canonical *counterexample* to (ii): a bounded prior gives saturation. The single state p is the whole trade history with no decay in units, so (i) is only supported in the weak sense that between trades p drifts (the absence of trades is informative). The no-manipulation side is exact (quotes are conditional expectations). Whether the intensity is monotone increasing in |v − p| was not read from the text. Verified (abstract; model and shape via secondary sources).

### Li, T. (2013). "Insider Trading with Uncertain Informed Trading." SSRN Working Paper 946324, City University of Hong Kong; first posted Nov 2006, latest version 2 Feb 2013; presented WFA/WFC/CICF 2012; no journal version found.
https://ssrn.com/abstract=946324 (DOI 10.2139/ssrn.946324)

Abstract (indexed SSRN text): "This paper develops a model of securities market with a strategic trader who is potentially better informed than the public. Unlike in the Kyle model (Kyle (1985) and Back (1992)), market makers do not have perfect information about whether the strategic trader is informed. With common prior beliefs, not only do market makers need to update their value estimate as in the Kyle model, but they also have to update their estimate of the probability that the strategic trader has private information based on the observed cumulative order flow. The dynamics of this probability estimate causes the pricing rule to be stochastic, and so do the market depth and price volatility. The expected profit of the strategic trader is lower with lower prior probability that she is informed, thus, the model is robust." Keywords include "Zakai Equation" and "Probability of Informed Trading". Relation to us: this is the direct antecedent of (i) and (ii) in one model: a posterior on "is an informed trader present" driven by cumulative flow, which modulates depth, so the response to cumulative flow steepens as the presence posterior rises; but the informed type is fixed over the horizon (no switching off), so the posterior does not decay when flow stops except through the growth of noise variance with elapsed time, and the state is cumulative rather than decayed flow. Body not accessible (SSRN blocks fetches); the closest fully verified formalization is Back–Crotty–Li 2018 below. Not fully verified.

### Banerjee, S. and Green, B. (2015). "Signal or Noise? Uncertainty and Learning about Whether Other Traders Are Informed." *Journal of Financial Economics* 117(2), 398–423.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2015.05.003 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=2139771 ; 2014 draft https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=1029

Primitives (read from the 2014 draft): a competitive CARA-normal rational-expectations market, not a market-maker model; dividend D = μ + d with d ~ N(0, σ²); a group θ that is either informed (sees d + ε) or trades on an independent noise signal, with prior π₀ = Pr(informed); uninformed speculators U cannot tell which, so their beliefs are a mixture of normals whose variance rises with S². Proposition 1: P = (1/R)[μ + (κ + (1 − κ)π₀)λS − κασ²(1 − λ)Z] with κ = var_U/(var_U + var_θ) "U-shaped in S around 0"; the weight on the informed-type mean rises with |S|, so the slope of the expectations component rises from below λ at S = 0 to λ as |S| → ∞ (odd, steepening, asymptotically linear, no saturation because d is Gaussian; this shape is our reading of their formula, the paper itself says only "non-linear" and "asymmetric"). Propositions 2–3: dP/dS(s) < dP/dS(−s) for s > 0 because the risk premium rises in |S|; the dynamic version gives volatility clustering and the leverage effect. Relation to us: supports the *shape* of (ii) from presence uncertainty (variance-weighting gives shrink-near-zero and full follow-through far out) and the idea that surprises raise the next update (a cousin of (iii)); it is a risk-averse REE, not a posterior-mean price, so it says nothing about loops, and it is not driven by order flow, so nothing about (i). Verified.

### Back, K., Crotty, K. and Li, T. (2018). "Identifying Information Asymmetry in Securities Markets." *Review of Financial Studies* 31(6), 2277–2325.
https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhx133 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=2565216 ; 2017 draft https://www.cb.cityu.edu.hk/ef/doc/2017%20finance%20conf/Tao%20Li.pdf

Primitives (read from the 2017 draft): horizon [0, 1]; one risk-neutral strategic trader who with probability α receives a signal S ∈ {L, H} (L < 0 < H, Pr(L | event) = p_L) and with probability 1 − α knows there is no event and then trades as a zero-profit contrarian; Brownian noise flow with standard deviation σ; market makers see Y_t and price P_t = V_t + p(t, Y_t), p = E[ξS | F_t]. Theorem 1: p(t, y) = L·N((y_L − y)/(σ√(1 − t))) + H·N((y − y_H)/(σ√(1 − t))) with thresholds y_L = σN⁻¹(αp_L), y_H = σN⁻¹(1 − αp_H); the informed rate is a Brownian-bridge drift that drives Y₁ into the right tail with probability one, Y is a Brownian motion in the market makers' filtration, and p solves the backward heat equation with a three-step terminal condition. Theorem 2: λ(t, y) = ∂p/∂y is a martingale, largest near the thresholds, not monotone in |y|. The text: "the price is more sensitive to orders when α is larger"; "if the likelihood [of an event] is judged to be high, then prices are quite sensitive to orders", otherwise "relatively insensitive" (Figure 1, α ∈ {0.1, 0.5}: smaller α widens the flat band). Relation to us: the cleanest verified support for the *mechanism* of (ii): a sparse three-point prior (mass 1 − α at zero) plus presence uncertainty yields a dead zone near zero and steepening toward the thresholds, exactly "shrink near zero, follow far out"; but the bounded support makes it saturate beyond y_H, so to keep steepening one needs the jump-size prior unbounded (Back 1992). The state is cumulative flow, no decay (against (i)); λ grows with displacement only up to the threshold, qualified support for (iii); posterior-mean price, so no loops. Verified.

## 3. Thematic searches

### 3A. Convex (steepening) price response from heavy tails or uncertain presence

Verdict from the papers above and the additional ones below: the sign of the curvature of a posterior-mean price in cumulative flow is decided by the tail of the *value-jump* prior relative to the noise (Back 1992's quantile transform, Bagnoli–Viswanathan–Holden's matching condition), and by whether that support is bounded. Presence uncertainty with a bounded jump gives a dead zone then saturation (Back–Crotty–Li 2018; Avery–Zemsky 1998; Çetin–Lin 2026); presence uncertainty with a Gaussian jump gives steepening to an asymptotic line (Banerjee–Green 2015's weighting); a heavier-than-Gaussian jump gives steepening without bound (Back 1992). Heavy tails in the *size* of informed flow, by contrast, give concave impact (Farmer et al. 2013), and so do liquidity-geometry mechanisms (Tóth et al. 2011; Nadtochiy 2022). No paper states "convex iff heavier-than-noise value tail" as a theorem; it is an immediate reading of Back's map that the brief's Part A should prove for the special case.

### 3B. Transient impact that decays because the posterior of an informed regime decays

Verdict: the literal mechanism ("the alarm falls in quiet time because an active informed trader would have traded") is Easley–O'Hara 1992 and its Poisson version EKOP 1996, where the log-odds of an event fall linearly in elapsed time at rate μ and jump by a constant per signed trade, so the *odds* decay exponentially at the informed intensity. Filters for Markov-modulated point processes (episodes that start and stop at Poisson rates) give a posterior that relaxes toward the stationary law between arrivals along an ODE and jumps at arrivals; exponential relaxation is the small-alarm linearization. **No paper was found that derives an exponentially weighted moving average of signed flow, in units, as the sufficient statistic for a posterior over an informed regime**; the two closest constructions are a Gaussian one (a hidden mean-reverting drift observed through Gaussian flow, whose Kalman posterior mean is exactly an EWMA of flow: Vodret et al. 2021 in stationary Kyle form) and an assumed one (Cartea–Jaimungal–Ricci 2014's short-term alpha).

"Liquidity, Information, and Infrequently Traded Stocks." *Journal of Finance* 51(4), 1405–1436. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb04074.x. The PIN model: an event with probability α per day, bad with probability δ, informed orders at rate μ, uninformed buys and sells at rate ε each. The log-odds of "good event vs no event" after B buys over elapsed time t is −μt + B·log(1 + μ/ε): a cumulative signed count minus a linear clock-time drift, so the odds are (1 + μ/ε)^B e^{−μt}, exponential decay at rate μ in the *odds*, and the sufficient statistic is (B, S, t). Verified (standard model structure; page not re-fetched).

## 4. Feature-by-feature verdict

| Feature | Standard result (cite) | Partial / different form | Novel in our model (nobody has it) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) alarm decays when flow stops, because an active informed trader would have traded | Easley–O'Hara 1992; EKOP 1996 (odds of an event decay at the informed rate μ in quiet time); Back–Baruch 2004 (time between trades is informative because the informed intensity depends on the posterior) | Markov-modulated point-process filters (Bayraktar–Ludkovski 2009; Campi–Zabaljauregui 2020) give an ODE relaxation between arrivals with exponential rate in the small-alarm limit; episodes that start/stop at random (Kelly–Steigerwald 2004; Caldentey–Stacchetti 2010 for the stop) | a single exponentially weighted net flow *in units* as the sufficient statistic. "EWMA + steepening T" is therefore an approximation that Part A must justify (small-alarm regime, or a Laplace/three-point jump prior with Poisson arrivals). |

The brief's claim "the state φ decaying in units is Alfonsi–Schied's volume-resilience model, and their no-manipulation result is our M0" is confirmed, with the precise hypotheses: shape function nonincreasing away from the touch on each side (equivalently T convex on ℝ₊, odd), unbounded cumulative depth, exponential volume reversion at any deterministic or stochastic rate, finitely many trades at arbitrary times; conclusion: no standard and no transaction-triggered price manipulation, and the cost-minimizing execution never alternates sign (AS 2010, Assumption 2.9, Proposition 2.10, Corollary 2.12, Remark 3.2). Their assumption (b) also covers concave power laws, so M0 does not need steepening for safety; steepening is an information requirement, not a safety one.

Two things the literature says that the operator should hear:

1. Gatheral 2010 is not a threat to M0 but is a threat to any *implementation* that decays the price displacement rather than the volume state: if a future version stores "skew in price" and decays it exponentially while keeping T nonlinear, Gatheral's slow-in/fast-out round trip is profitable. The safe object to decay is φ in units.
2. The empirical impact literature (square-root law) measures something our T does not model: average metaorder impact over its own decay. A convex T predicts convex burst-level impact from the AMM's fills alone; if that is ever measured on the venue it will look unlike listed markets, and the only theoretical defense is Back 1992's (value tails heavier than noise) or Çetin–Waelbroeck's (signal tail index below 2). Farmer et al. 2013 show that heavy tails in *flow size* push the other way.

## 5. Papers closest to the whole model

1. **Alfonsi and Schied (2010)**: the mechanics. Volume-decaying state, general shape function, infinite depth, exponential resilience at any rate, and the theorem that this admits no price manipulation. Everything in M0 is there except the information story and the permanent updates.
2. **Easley and O'Hara (1992)** with its Poisson form **EKOP (1996)**: the information story for τ. Uncertain informed presence, one-directional flow raising the event posterior, quiet time lowering it at the informed intensity, a transient component (presence) and a permanent one (realized value). Missing: decay in units, restart of episodes, an unbounded jump, and a convex response.
3. **Back, Crotty and Li (2018)** (with Li 2013 as the continuous-time antecedent): the shape of T from primitives. Presence uncertainty plus a sparse jump prior, Kyle-type posterior-mean pricing on cumulative flow, an explicit pricing function that is flat near zero and steepens with displacement, and a λ(t, y) that grows with displacement over the relevant range (our |T(φ)|-scaled update). Missing: decay (the state is cumulative flow) and an unbounded jump (it saturates).

Back–Baruch 2004 is the right *special case to solve* for Part A (Poisson noise per side, quotes including the fill, informed intensity depending on mispricing), provided the binary value is replaced by a three-point or Laplace jump and the informed trader is present only during Poisson episodes; Guéant 2013 is the reference for the S′(P) floor; Vodret et al. 2021 is the Gaussian benchmark in which the EWMA is exactly the posterior mean and T is exactly linear.

## 7. Equilibrium price impact and manipulation-proofness

Throughout, (E), (B), (N) are the brief's three properties: (E) the rule is the pricing rule of a strategic equilibrium; (B) fill prices are conditional expectations in the market maker's fill-inclusive filtration; (N) no sure-profit loop, ideally with unbounded positions.

Relation between the notions (Gatheral–Schied 2013, Proposition 1, taken from Klöck–Schied–Sun): no negative expected liquidation costs ⇒ no price manipulation; if buys raise and sells lower the price, no transaction-triggered manipulation ⇒ no negative expected liquidation costs ⇒ no price manipulation. For a deterministic AMM the expected and sure versions coincide, so our (N) is exactly "no price manipulation" in the Huberman–Stanzl sense, and the storage-function characterization in M4-SAFETY.md is the AMM version of "no negative liquidation costs" (cash out of any state is bounded by what is stored in it).

### 7.1 Reduced-form large-trader models: the no-arbitrage theorems

These papers take the pricing rule as given (a "reduced-form" or "reaction" function) and prove (N) from a structural condition plus no-arbitrage for small traders. None of them has (E) or (B) in the brief's sense; they are the mathematical-finance side of deliverable J.

#### Jarrow, R. A. (1992). "Market Manipulation, Bubbles, Corners, and Short Squeezes." *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 27(3), 311–336.
https://doi.org/10.2307/2331322 (RePEc: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jfinqa:v:27:y:1992:i:03:p:311-336_00 ; reprinted in *Financial Derivatives Pricing: Selected Works of Robert Jarrow*, World Scientific 2008, ch. 6, https://doi.org/10.1142/9789812819222_0006)

Primitives: discrete time, finite horizon, a stock and a money market account; one *large trader* ("any investor whose trades change prices") and price-taking small traders; a reduced-form price process that may depend on the large trader's current holdings *and on his past holdings* (history dependence, "price momentum": a purchase at one date raises prices at later dates even after the trader stops). The trader's "paper wealth" is his position marked at the price his own holdings support; his "real wealth" is the cash after liquidating to zero holdings. A market manipulation strategy "generates positive real wealth with no risk" (abstract): exactly our sure-profit loop. Results (abstract plus three citing accounts): manipulation strategies "exist under reasonable hypotheses on the equilibrium price process": with price momentum (buy, let the momentum lift the price, unwind), with corners (the trader holds more than the float and squeezes shorts), and in the 1994 companion with derivatives. Sufficient conditions for nonexistence are "properties of the reduced-form price function" (Chakraborty–Yilmaz's account): Bank–Baum 2004 state it as "absence of arbitrage for the large investor under the assumption of absence of arbitrage for small investors in periods where the large investor does not trade", and Çetin–Jarrow–Protter 2004 identify the structural part as the absence of the history dependence ("the elimination of this path dependency condition in our structure excludes market manipulation"). Goldstein–Guembel 2008 give the mechanism: without momentum "the speculator's attempt to unwind his original trade in a later date will have a symmetric price impact." Verdict: (E) no (the price process is exogenous, called "equilibrium" but not derived); (B) no; (N) yes, as a theorem, under "price depends on current holdings only (no memory) and the small-trader market is arbitrage-free", and *false* with memory. Relation to us: our decaying state φ is memory of past trades, so Jarrow's sufficient condition does not cover us; what replaces it is the storage function (7.1, Bank–Baum) generalized to a state with autonomous evolution, which is M4-SAFETY's route. Unbounded positions: yes (no cap; corners need a finite float, which an AMM with infinite depth does not have). Citation verified; the theorem's exact wording reconstructed from Bank–Baum 2004 (p. 2), Çetin–Jarrow–Protter 2004 (p. 4) and Goldstein–Guembel 2008 (p. 134), the JFQA text itself not read. (1995), "Market manipulation", in Jarrow, Maksimovic, Ziemba (eds), *Handbooks in OR & MS* vol. 9 (Finance), 611–630, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0927-0507(05)80064-7 (abstract page only).

#### Bank, P. and Baum, D. (2004). "Hedging and Portfolio Optimization in Financial Markets with a Large Trader." *Mathematical Finance* 14(1), 1–18.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0960-1627.2004.00179.x ; author copy https://www.math.cmu.edu/~bachelier/seminar_4s/BankBaum_IlliquidMarkets.pdf

Primitives (read from the author copy): a smooth family of continuous semimartingales P(ϑ, t), ϑ ∈ ℝ, "the asset price when the large investor holds ϑ shares" (the Frey–Stremme reaction-function model generalized); the investor's semimartingale strategy θ; bank account dβ = −P(θ_{−}, t) dθ − d[P(θ, ·), θ], so a block order Δθ is filled at the *post-order* price P(θ_t, t) (fill-inclusive). Assumption 2: P is nondecreasing in ϑ ("crucial for our model to exclude trivial arbitrage": otherwise an in-and-out trade earns {P(ϑ) − P(ϑ′)}{ϑ′ − ϑ} > 0). Real wealth V^θ = β + L(θ, t) with L(ϑ, t) = ∫₀^ϑ P(x, t) dx, the proceeds of a fast liquidation. Lemma 3.2 (Itô–Wentzell): dV^θ = L(θ_{−}, dt) − ½ P′(θ_{−}, t) d[θ]^c − Σ ∫_{θ_{−}}^{θ} {P(θ, t) − P(x, t)} dx, i.e. exogenous shocks minus nonnegative impact costs that vanish for continuous finite-variation strategies. Assumption 3: one measure P* under which *every* P^ϑ is a local martingale (stronger than no-arbitrage for small investors, which only gives a measure per ϑ). Theorem 2.3: under Assumptions 1–3 there is no arbitrage for the large investor in the class of admissible strategies (the proof: V^θ ≤ V₀ + ∫ L(θ_{−}, ds), a P*-supermartingale). Verdict: (E) no; (B) partial: prices are martingales under a *pricing* measure common to all inventory levels, not posteriors of any information; (N) yes, with unbounded positions, the state being the current inventory only. Relation to us: this is the storage-function theorem for the memoryless special case: L(ϑ, t) is our G, monotonicity of P is convexity of G, and Assumption 3 is what a random fair F needs (the same measure for every position: the market price of risk must not depend on the large trader's position). The transient state φ is outside the model (the price effect of an order "will persist until the next order makes asset prices follow a possibly different dynamics", no relaxation). Verified from the full text.

Primitives (read from the author copy): a stochastic *supply curve* S(t, x, ω), the price per share for an order of size x at time t, nondecreasing and C² in x, with S(·, 0) a semimartingale; traders are price takers with respect to the curve, so "the investor's trading strategy has no lasting impact on the price process": the curve depends on the current order only, not on past orders (p. 4: this "excludes market manipulation"; extending to history dependence is left to "future research"). Self-financing with liquidity cost L_t ≥ 0, nondecreasing, zero for continuous finite-variation strategies (Lemma 2.1). Definition 3.1: an arbitrage is a self-financing strategy with zero initial wealth, nonnegative terminal wealth and positive probability of positive terminal wealth. Theorem 3.1: if S(·, 0) has an equivalent local martingale measure there is no arbitrage; Theorem 3.2 (first fundamental theorem): NFLVR holds iff S(·, 0) has an ELMM; the arbitrage-free price of a claim equals its classical price (traders avoid liquidity costs by trading continuously). Footnote 1 contrasts with Bank–Baum: "in Bank and Baum a manipulative-free economy requires an equivalent measure such that every point on the supply curve evolves as a martingale. In our model, an arbitrage free economy requires only an equivalent measure such that the supply curve evaluated at the zero net trade evolves as a martingale." Verdict: (E) no; (B) partial (the marginal price at zero trade is a martingale under a pricing measure); (N) yes, but by fiat: with no history dependence a round trip's only cost is the walk up the curve and back, which is nonnegative by monotonicity. Relation to us: this is the "M0 with τ = 0" limit (instant full resilience, T(φ) with φ reset after every fill), and it shows the fundamental theorem's shape when the state carries no memory: (N) ⟺ the zero-trade price is a martingale under some equivalent measure. With memory (our φ, Roch–Soner's ℓ) an extra condition on the memory dynamics appears, which is the next entry. Verified from the full text.

#### Roch, A. and Soner, H. M. (2013). "Resilient Price Impact of Trading and the Cost of Illiquidity." *International Journal of Theoretical and Applied Finance* 16(6), 1350037 (27 pp.).
https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219024913500374 ; preprint (29 March 2011) https://www.epfl.ch/schools/cdm/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Soner.pdf ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=1923840

Primitives (read from the preprint): a block-shaped book with stochastic depth 1/(2m_t) and stochastic bid–ask tightness; an "equilibrium price" S^e (the price with no trading); the trader's cumulative trades z; the mid-quote displacement ℓ = S − S^e follows dℓ_t = −κ_t ℓ_t dt + 2m_{t−} dz_t + 2d[m, z]_t (eq. 4.2; price-impact reversion at rate κ_t, allowed to depend on the state). Remark 4.1: with λ(t) = 2m_t, u = z, v = S^e and the insider's linear feedback β this is Kyle 1985's continuous equilibrium, dℓ = −κ ℓ dt + 2m dz with κ = βλ, i.e. the decay of impact is the insider's trading against the mispricing. Liquidity cost L_t (eq. 4.9): Theorem 4.2: L_t ≥ 0 pathwise if m is constant; in general E[L_t] ≥ 0 if Λ²_t/m_t is a local supermartingale, Λ_t = exp(−∫₀ᵗ κ). Theorem 1 (Section 4): if under some Q ∼ P the process S^e is a local martingale and Λ²/m is a supermartingale, there are no arbitrage opportunities (wealth bounded below, terminal wealth ≥ 0 a.s. and > 0 with positive probability). The extension "some part of the cumulative trade impacts ℓ_t do not decay with time so that the process S converges to a secondary process S⁰ which includes permanent impacts proportional to ℓ_t" is mentioned and dropped as offering "no new insights". Verdict: (E) no (though Remark 4.1 shows the impact law is Kyle's equilibrium in reduced form); (B) partial (S^e a Q-local martingale); (N) yes for constant depth with any κ_t (the pathwise statement is a storage function ℓ²/(4m)), and with stochastic depth iff, in expectation, depth does not rise faster than the transient decays squared: m_t ≥ m_s e^{−2∫_s^t κ}. The elementary round trip (buy x at s, sell at t) has revenue x²[m_s(2e^{−∫κ} − 1) − m_t], and (1 − e^{−a})² ≥ 0 shows the theorem's condition is the natural sufficient one. Relation to us: the exact price-impact-reversion analogue of M0's linear case, with the warning that a liquidity state that *rises* (density thickening) while a displacement is still decaying is pumpable; in our model the density is a function of φ, so this is the "T′ must not fall as φ decays" content of the storage condition, not an exogenous clock effect. Verified from the preprint.

#### Gatheral, J. and Schied, A. (2013). "Dynamical Models of Market Impact and Algorithms for Order Execution." In Fouque, J.-P. and Langsam, J. A. (eds), *Handbook on Systemic Risk*, Cambridge University Press, ch. 22, 579–602.

Survey (read from the SSRN text). Standing assumption (1): the unaffected price S⁰ is a martingale. Definitions 1–3 and Proposition 1 as in 7.0. #### Fruth, A., Schöneborn, T. and Urusov, M. (2014). "Optimal Trade Execution and Price Manipulation in Order Books with Time-Varying Liquidity." *Mathematical Finance* 24(4), 651–695.
https://doi.org/10.1111/mafi.12022 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.2631 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=1925808

Primitives (read from the ar5iv text up to Section 7; Section 8 not rendered): Obizhaeva–Wang block book with deterministic time-dependent depth q_t and resilience ρ_t; a buy order ξ_t moves the ask by ξ_t/q_t, split into a permanent part γξ_t and a transient part K_t ξ_t with γ + K_t = 1/q_t, the transient part decaying as K_t e^{−∫_t^s ρ} ξ_t; unaffected bid and ask are H¹-martingales. Two variants: (i) a *trading-dependent spread*: a buy moves the ask at once but the bid only through the permanent part γ(1 − e^{−∫ρ})ξ_t, so the spread widens with one's own trading; (ii) zero spread. Relation to us: our density 1/T′(φ) is a *state-dependent* depth, and the alarm makes it thin when displaced and thick at rest, which is the safe direction (depth rises only as the displacement has already decayed); the paper is the reference for deliverable L's "adding a spread" option: a spread that widens with one's own flow removes manipulation for free, at the price of the wait region. Also the model behind Kyle–Viswanathan's "intertemporal market depth arbitrage" (7.2). Partially verified.

#### Klöck, F., Schied, A. and Sun, Y. (2017). "Price manipulation in a market impact model with dark pool." *Applied Mathematical Finance* 24(5), 417–450.
https://doi.org/10.1080/1350486X.2017.1406438 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.4008

Primitives (read from the arXiv text): an Almgren–Chriss exchange (permanent γ, temporary h, martingale unaffected price P⁰) plus a dark pool in which the trader's resting order X̂ is matched against Poisson-arriving orders at the exchange mid-price with slippage β(·); dark fills have a fraction α ∈ [0, 1] of the permanent impact they would have had at the exchange and a temporary cross-venue impact κ h(ξ) from the trader's concurrent exchange trading. Proposition 3.4 is the hierarchy quoted in 7.0. Theorem 4.1: for all Almgren–Chriss models and all horizons, no price manipulation (equivalently nonnegative expected liquidation costs) iff α = 1, κ = 0 and |β(y)| ≥ (γ/2)|y|: dark fills must carry *full* permanent impact, no temporary cross-impact, and be penalized by at least half the permanent-impact potential of the executed size. With α = 0 (dark fills invisible to the exchange price) there is always manipulation for a large enough horizon (Propositions 4.7–4.10), realized only by transaction-triggered strategies that push the exchange quote and get filled in the dark (Proposition 4.11); the critical horizon is T* = 2η/γ for linear temporary impact (Proposition 4.8). Verdict: (E) no, (B) no, (N) a complete characterization for two venues. Relation to us: the "second venue" is any place where the same asset trades at the AMM's marginal price without paying the AMM's stored potential: an external reference price that F tracks, a fee-free fill at F, or a cash-settled contract on the AMM's price (Kumar–Seppi in 7.2). The condition |β| ≥ γ|y|/2 is precisely "the other venue must charge the potential S(P + y) − S(P)", i.e. every fill anywhere must be charged through the same storage function. Verified.

#### Abernethy, J., Chen, Y. and Wortman Vaughan, J. (2013). "Efficient Market Making via Convex Optimization, and a Connection to Online Learning." *ACM Transactions on Economics and Computation* 1(2), Art. 12, 1–39.

### 7.2 Equilibrium models: manipulation as a strategy that the market prices

#### Allen, F. and Gale, D. (1992). "Stock-Price Manipulation." *Review of Financial Studies* 5(3), 503–529.
https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/5.3.503 (RePEc: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:5:y:1992:i:3:p:503-29)

Primitives (abstract and citing papers): three dates; a large trader who is, with some probability, informed (he knows the firm is undervalued or a takeover is coming) and otherwise an uninformed manipulator; a continuum of risk-averse, expected-utility-maximizing investors in a rational-expectations equilibrium (no risk-neutral competitive market maker); the informed type is impatient and sells at date 2, and the manipulator can mimic him. Result: "in a rational expectations framework, where all agents maximize expected utility, it is possible for an uninformed manipulator to make a profit, provided investors attach a positive probability to the manipulator being an informed trader" (abstract), in a pooling equilibrium with "no price momentum and no possibility of a corner" (Aggarwal–Wu's account). Prices rise during accumulation and fall on revelation. Huberman–Stanzl's reading: "This price manipulation does not erode the equilibrium because traders are volume-constrained." Chakraborty–Yılmaz's reading (working paper, p. 2): "In their model, manipulation by the informed trader is not profitable, although manipulation by an uninformed trader is profitable in the presence of certain restrictions on the strategy of the informed trader" (the informed type is made to sell at date 2, so the manipulator can copy him). Verdict: (E) yes, with a restricted informed strategy; (B) no (prices carry risk premia and are not a risk-neutral maker's posterior); (N) fails in expectation; not a sure profit (the manipulator's profit is the informed type's rent, earned on average against risk-averse investors). Relation to us: the pooling channel needs (a) the manipulator to be indistinguishable from the informed, (b) an informed type whose strategy is exogenously fixed rather than best-responding, and (c) a counterparty that pays a premium for the pooled flow; a risk-neutral posterior-mean maker facing a best-responding informed trader satisfies (a) and denies (b) and (c), which is why the Kyle/GM versions below come out the other way. Verified (abstract); model details from Aggarwal–Wu 2006, Huberman–Stanzl 2004, Chakraborty–Yılmaz's working paper and Goldstein–Guembel 2008.

#### Allen, F. and Gorton, G. (1992). "Stock price manipulation, market microstructure and asymmetric information." *European Economic Review* 36(2–3), 624–630.
https://doi.org/10.1016/0014-2921(92)90120-L ; NBER WP 3862 https://www.nber.org/papers/w3862

Primitives (abstract): Glosten–Milgrom sequential trade with a specialist quoting conditional expectations; liquidity *sellers* are forced (cash needs), liquidity *buyers* choose their time and cluster, so outside the clustering time a purchase is more likely informed than a sale: "the price movement resulting from a purchase is larger than for a sale." Result: "profitable manipulation by uninformed investors may occur. A model where the specialist takes account of the possibility of manipulation in equilibrium is presented." The round trip is buy-buy-...-sell-sell: the asks step up on each buy, the bids barely move on each sell, so the manipulator sells near the top what he bought on the way up, *if* the specialist prices sells as liquidity-driven. Chakraborty–Yılmaz (working paper, p. 2) report the equilibrium outcome: "In every equilibrium of their model, the uninformed manipulator makes zero profits." So the profit exists against a specialist who ignores the manipulator and is competed to zero once the specialist's quotes include him (the bid after a run of buys falls by the probability that the seller is the manipulator unloading). Verdict: (E) yes, with the manipulator priced; (B) yes (every quote is a conditional expectation including the fill); (N) in expectation: the manipulator earns zero in every equilibrium, and the losses of the forced liquidity sellers pay for the pooling. Not addressed: sure profits, or what happens to the manipulator's *conditional* profit once his own past fills are known to him but not to the specialist. Relation to us: the published instance of the brief's "misattributed fills" issue and of its cure: with asymmetric flow the naive posterior-mean quotes are exploitable, and the equilibrium quotes that include the manipulator's strategy are not (in expectation). The AMM analogue is that the response to a sell after a run of buys must be as large as the response to the buys, i.e. a symmetric (odd) T on a state that remembers the run, which is what T(φ) does mechanically and what the equilibrium specialist does through his belief about who is selling. Verified (abstract; the zero-profit statement from Chakraborty–Yılmaz's working paper; the specialist's equilibrium itself not read: the journal text is blocked and the NBER scan has no text layer).

#### Chakraborty, A. and Yılmaz, B. (2004). "Manipulation in market order models." *Journal of Financial Markets* 7(2), 187–206.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2003.11.001 (RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v7y2004i2p187-206.html); companion "Informed Manipulation", *JET* 114(1), 132–152, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-0531(03)00101-7 (Section 3A above); working paper https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a1851839-704f-4f6c-b299-032739f0cbc7/content

Primitives (abstract and introduction): a dynamic Kyle market-order model with finitely many trade sizes, competitive market makers pricing at E[v | flow], noise traders of arbitrary distribution, and a long-lived insider who is present with probability less than one. Result: "when the market faces uncertainty about the existence of the insider in the market, the equilibrium outcome changes in a significant way. In particular, the insider manipulates (i.e., trades in the wrong direction and undertakes short term losses) in every equilibrium, given a long enough horizon, and independently of the precise nature of noise trading"; the same in a GM setting in the JET paper. The introduction states the benchmark: in Kyle 1985 "the equilibrium does not involve manipulation of prices"; the JET working paper (read) explains why: "in the linear equilibrium of Kyle's model, the informed trader's trading strategy is monotonic in his information in the sense that he buys the asset when the asset is undervalued given his information ... and sells the asset when the asset is overvalued", and presence uncertainty is what breaks monotonicity. The same working paper is the source for the readings of Jarrow ("sufficient conditions ... are properties of the 'reduced-form' price function", one "in which the response of the market to the large trader's trades has already been incorporated, so that it is a function only of the large trader's trades"), of Allen–Gale and of Allen–Gorton quoted in their entries; the authors' own model has no uninformed strategic trader ("whereas Jarrow looks at manipulation by an uninformed trader, in this paper we look at informed manipulation"). Takayama (2021, *JEDC* 125, 104086, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jedc.2021.104086) proves uniqueness in the GM version and that the unique equilibrium has bid and ask monotone in the maker's belief and involves manipulation by the insider when the horizon is long. Verdict: (E) yes; (B) yes; (N) for uninformed loops follows from posterior-mean quotes (no uninformed player is modelled), while the informed trader's "manipulation" is a priced mixed strategy, not a money pump. Relation to us: presence uncertainty (our alarm) is exactly what makes the informed trader pump the presence posterior; the equilibrium prices it, so no uninformed loop appears, but the pricing rule is then a function of the whole belief, not of (φ, P, F) (F-attack.md Theorem 4). Verified (abstract, introduction excerpts, JET working paper text); the JFM text itself not read.

#### Kyle, A. S. and Viswanathan, S. (2008). "How to Define Illegal Price Manipulation." *American Economic Review* 98(2), 274–279.
https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.98.2.274 ; author PDF https://people.duke.edu/~viswanat/pp-article-kyle_vish_manipulation_20080100_f.pdf

Read in full. Definition: a strategy is illegal manipulation only if it "simultaneously undermines both pricing accuracy and market liquidity"; this "stands in sharp contrast to unsatisfactory definitions based on the routine rational exploitation of market power or private information necessarily part of a Bayesian Nash equilibrium." Benign strategies listed: restricting quantity to avoid impact (Kyle 1985, 1989), mixed strategies "which involve both buying and selling to minimize transactions costs (Foster and Viswanathan 1994, Back and Baruch 2004)", bluffing (van Bommel 2003), "inter-temporal market depth arbitrage (Black 1995)", and punching the close (Kumar–Seppi 1992's cash-settlement strategy, reinterpreted as equivalent to taking delivery). On depth: "Kyle (1985) presents a model where in equilibrium, market depth must be constant because, if it is not, a trader can make large profits inconsistent with equilibrium by trading very aggressively either to add noise to prices or to make prices very accurate." Goldstein–Guembel's feedback short-selling is classified as illegal manipulation; predatory trading (Brunnermeier–Pedersen) is "different from non-manipulative but illegal front-running" and hard to distinguish from depth arbitrage; for pure trade-based schemes "it may be difficult or impractical for the legal system to define and enforce". Verdict: no model; the paper is the statement that in a Bayesian Nash equilibrium bluffing and randomizing are priced and therefore not exploitable, and the "constant depth" remark is the one-line version of "E ⇒ no intertemporal depth arbitrage": an equilibrium pricing rule cannot have a time profile of depth that a round trip could exploit, because the round trip would be a profitable deviation. Relation to us: this is the E-side argument the brief wants to make precise for the *state*-dependent depth 1/T′(φ): a depth profile indexed by the trader's own displacement is not exploitable in the same way as one indexed by the clock, which is Roch–Soner's and Fruth et al.'s dividing line. Verified.

#### Goldstein, I. and Guembel, A. (2008). "Manipulation and the Allocational Role of Prices." *Review of Economic Studies* 75(1), 133–164.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-937X.2007.00467.x ; author copy https://finance.wharton.upenn.edu/~itayg/Files/manipulation-published.pdf

Read in full. Primitives: dates 0–3; one risk-neutral speculator who at date 0 is positively informed, negatively informed, or uninformed (he knows which); two trading rounds (t = 1, 2) with orders in {−1, 0, 1}; noise traders; a risk-neutral market maker who "starts with the expectation that the value of the firm is V⁺/2 and updates this expectation after every round of trade" (Kyle-style batch pricing, E[V | order flow]); at date 3 the firm invests or not. Section 3, no-feedback benchmark (the firm knows the state): Lemma 1 characterizes the informed types (they may mix between trading and waiting at t = 1 to reduce their price impact); Proposition 1: "In any equilibrium of the no-feedback game, the uninformed speculator never trades in t = 1", with proof: buying at t = 1 executes at p₁(1), p₁(2) > V⁺/2 (informed types buy with positive probability, so a buy raises the price), unwinding at t = 2 executes at E[p₂ | sell] ≤ p₁ since the positively informed is expected to buy at t = 2 and a sell pushes the price down, so the round trip loses. The text then adds the qualification that matters for us: "the uninformed speculator may trade in t = 2 in equilibrium. This happens when at t = 1 there is a non-zero noise trade. In this case, the speculator, although uninformed about the future liquidation value, has an informational advantage over the market maker, since he knows that he did not trade, and that the t = 1 order flow was due to noise. The market maker, who does not know that, adjusts the price to reflect a positive probability that the speculator did trade." With feedback (Section 4): Proposition 2 exhibits an equilibrium in which the uninformed short-sells at t = 1 and profits, because his sell lowers the price, the firm cancels the investment, the value falls, and he covers cheaply; Proposition 3: for a strong feedback effect every equilibrium has this; Section 6: buy-side manipulation is never profitable (the asymmetry comes from the option-like investment decision). Verdict: (E) yes (manipulator as a player); (B) yes; (N): the *round trip* by the uninformed is unprofitable in every equilibrium without feedback (Proposition 1 is the closest published theorem of the form "equilibrium with Bayesian pricing ⇒ uninformed manipulation unprofitable"), but a strategy that conditions on his own past (non-)trades has positive expected profit, and with feedback from price to value the round trip is profitable. Relation to us: Proposition 1's proof uses exactly the brief's ingredients (fill-inclusive batch prices, zero-profit maker, the manipulator's type in the support of the maker's beliefs) and nothing else, so it is the template for deliverable K; the t = 2 remark is the "policy consistency" gap made explicit (the maker cannot condition on who traded), and the feedback case is the "the AMM's price feeds back into the value" channel (a token whose treasury or collateral is marked at the AMM's price). Unbounded positions: no (unit orders). Verified.

#### Kumar, P. and Seppi, D. J. (1992). "Futures Manipulation with 'Cash Settlement'." *Journal of Finance* 47(4), 1485–1502. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04666.x — and Fishman, M. J. and Hagerty, K. M. (1995). "The Mandatory Disclosure of Trades and Market Liquidity." *Review of Financial Studies* 8(3), 637–676. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/8.3.637

Two Kyle-type models with competitive posterior-mean makers in which an uninformed trader earns positive expected profit: Kumar–Seppi through a cash-settled futures position whose payoff references the spot price the trader then pushes ("uninformed investors still earn positive expected profits by establishing a futures position and then trading in the spot market to manipulate the spot price used to compute the cash settlement"; profits vanish as the number of manipulators grows), Fishman–Hagerty through mandatory disclosure of an insider's trades when "the market cannot observe whether an insider is trading on information or not." #### Brunnermeier, M. K. and Pedersen, L. H. (2005). "Predatory Trading." *Journal of Finance* 60(4), 1825–1863.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2005.00781.x ; author copy https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/predatory_trading.pdf

Read in full. Primitives: I risk-neutral strategic traders with position limits x̄ and a trading-intensity limit A; long-term investors with a static downward-sloping demand, so the market-clearing price is p(t) = μ − λ(S − X(t)) with X the strategic traders' aggregate holdings (eq. 6); trading faster than A costs a temporary impact G. The long-term investors' demand "depends only on the current price p. That is, they do not attempt to profit from price swings" (p. 1832). A trader in distress must liquidate; the others know. Results: predators first sell alongside the prey, then buy back (price overshooting); "The distressed trader's excess liquidation cost equals the predator's profit from preying"; the long-term investors "are buying and selling shares at the same price" and are unaffected. The authors are explicit that this is not price manipulation: "The model is set up such that the trader cannot make or lose money based on the way his own trades affect prices. (For example, λ is assumed to be constant.) Rather, the trader makes money by exploiting the way in which the other traders affect prices (through X₋ᵢ). This distinguishes predatory trading from price manipulation." Verdict: (E) yes among the strategic traders, but the liquidity suppliers are not players and not Bayesian; (B) no; (N) yes for a single trader's own loop (linear permanent impact is a potential, temporary cost ≥ 0), and no for the market as a whole against predictable third-party flow. Relation to us: the exact failure mode of (B) that a decaying state invites: if the AMM's response to a *known* future flow (a scheduled liquidation, a decaying displacement that everyone can see) is not already in the price, the flow is front-run; a posterior-mean price of the fill-inclusive kind would have the prey's expected liquidation in it. Gatheral–Schied 2013 Section 5.2 and Schöneborn–Schied 2009 embed this into transient-impact models. Verified.

#### Back, K. and Baruch, S. (2004), manipulation angle (paper mapped in Section 2 above).
Kyle–Viswanathan's reading: "Back and Baruch (2004) show that randomizing between buying and selling may be an optimal way for a trader to extract as much liquidity from the market as possible"; the informed trader's bluffs are part of a mixed strategy that the market makers price, so quotes remain E[v | buy], E[v | sell] and no uninformed loop profits. The contrast with Chakraborty–Yılmaz: with the insider surely present, bluffing is a small-δ phenomenon and vanishes in the Kyle limit; with uncertain presence it is forced. Verdict: (E) yes, (B) yes, (N) yes for uninformed loops, with the informed trader's manipulation of the belief priced in; no clock decay of the state beyond the drift of p between trades. Nothing added to the verification status of Section 2.

#### Bank, P. and Kramkov, D. (2015). "A model for a large investor trading at market indifference prices. I: Single-period case." *Finance and Stochastics* 19(2), 449–472. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00780-015-0258-y ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.3224 — and "II: Continuous-time case." *Annals of Applied Probability* 25(5), 2708–2742. https://doi.org/10.1214/14-AAP1059 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.3229

Read from the arXiv texts. Primitives: M market makers with strictly concave utilities and a common filtration; a large investor whose orders are filled at the makers' *utility indifference prices*, after which the makers trade among themselves to a Pareto-optimal allocation of the total endowment (Part II: "the market makers compete with their quotes for the investor's orders and trade among themselves to attain Pareto optimal allocations"). The information channel is explicitly disregarded: F_t(Q) = F_t (Part II, Section 1), so all impact is inventory (risk-sharing) impact. Pricing rule: Part I Theorem 4.1: the cash cost x(q) of a position q is convex and C¹ with ∂x/∂q_j = −E^{Q(a(q))}[ψ_j], the expected payoff under the Pareto pricing measure induced by the post-trade allocation; Part II eq. (1.5): the marginal price is S_t(Q) = E^{Q_t(Q)}[ψ | F_t], a conditional expectation of the terminal payoff under a strategy-dependent measure; Theorem 4.2 (Part I) expresses the second-order impact through the makers' aggregate risk tolerance and the dispersion of individual risk tolerances. Absence of arbitrage: Part II Lemma 4.8: under Assumptions 2.1, 2.4, 4.2, if a strategy's terminal gain V_T (the cash after liquidation) satisfies V_T ≥ 0 then V_T = 0, and "contrary to the standard, small agent, model ... no further 'admissibility' conditions on a strategy Q are needed to exclude an arbitrage." The model also has the "closability" property (1.2) that the exogenous models lack: small strategies have small terminal cash. Verdict: (E) yes (competitive quotes from utility-maximizing makers with a Pareto reallocation); (B) yes in a modified sense: the marginal price is a conditional expectation of the payoff under an inventory-dependent equivalent measure, not the posterior of any information, and it is not a martingale under P; (N) yes, with unbounded positions and for every strategy. Relation to us: the only paper found in which (E) and (N) are proved together for a nonlinear price of the next unit; the mechanism is that the cash function is convex in the position (a storage function again: Part I's x(q) is G(P) with the pricing measure playing the role of the state), and the absence of a transient state is essential (impact is a function of the current inventory and the exogenous filtration). A decaying φ would correspond to makers whose risk tolerance recovers over time (capital arriving), which is a time-varying-depth extension of the kind Fruth et al. and Roch–Soner warn about. Verified.

#### Kyle, A. S., Obizhaeva, A. A. and Wang, Y. (2018). "Smooth Trading with Overconfidence and Market Power." *Review of Economic Studies* 85(1), 611–662.
https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdx017 ; SSRN https://ssrn.com/abstract=2423207 ; 2013 draft (read) https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=923

Primitives: N symmetric, oligopolistic, relatively overconfident traders with exponential utility who receive continuous Gaussian signals about a mean-reverting growth rate; no noise traders and no market makers ("There is only one type of trader"); traders submit demand schedules that are linear in price, own inventory and the *rate* of trading; a continuous double auction. Equilibrium (eqs. 67–68): each trader's target inventory is proportional to the gap between his own signal and the average of the others' signals inferred from prices, inventories move toward targets at the endogenous rate γ_S, and the price is a precision-weighted "dampened average" of all signals which "responds instantaneously to innovations". Implied price impact of a deviation (eqs. 71–72): P(t) = P*(t) + λ_S (S_n − S_n*) + λ_x (x_n − x_n*) with x_n = dS_n/dt: permanent impact linear in inventory, transitory impact linear in the *speed* of trading ("Almgren and Chriss (2000) use a functional form for price impact like equation (71) because of its computational simplicity. We show that this functional form arises endogenously in a model of informed trading"); block trades are infinitely expensive. Round trips: "If the new trader unwinds his position at a later date, the new trader recovers the permanent impact costs but incurs the temporary impact costs once again." Out-of-equilibrium liquidation at rate γ ≠ γ_S (eqs. 80–85): the expected price path is −(γ − γ_S)/((N − 1)γ_P) e^{−γt} S_n(T): the "transient impact with exponential decay" is the decay of the deviator's own trading rate as his inventory runs off, not a resilience of the book; five-times-faster liquidation produces a five-times-deeper "flash crash". The authors' own OW comparison: Obizhaeva–Wang 2013 "suggest an alternative model in which, rather than decaying instantaneously when a trader stops trading, temporary price impact decays gradually at an exponential rate." Martingale: "each trader believes that equilibrium prices usually differ from fundamentals, prices do not follow a martingale, and price changes are predictable" (each trader's own value estimate is a martingale, the others' are not, in his view). Verdict: (E) yes; (B) no (a dampened average of disagreeing posteriors, not any single agent's conditional expectation); (N) yes in the Almgren–Chriss sense for a single trader (Gatheral–Schied Proposition 3: linear permanent, convex temporary), with unbounded positions penalized by risk aversion; a manipulator as an extra player is not studied. Relation to us: this settles the brief's question about Kyle–Obizhaeva–Wang: its equilibrium impact has *no* transient state; the exponential in eq. (85) is the trader's own inventory decaying, so the model is not pumpable and not a counterexample to anything, but it is also not a microfoundation of a decaying φ. Verified from the 2013 draft.

#### Vodret, M., Mastromatteo, I., Tóth, B. and Benzaquen, M. (2021), manipulation angle (paper mapped in Section 3B above).
Read from the arXiv text. The market maker's rule is p_t = E[p^F_t | I^MM_t] with p^F_t the present value of future dividends (eq. 12, the "standard EMH fundamental rational expectation pricing-rule"); dividends are zero-mean and mean-reverting; the insider's infinite-horizon problem drops the round-trip constraint because "the expected price at infinity is zero". In the Markovian case "the price process ... is a discrete Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with timescale τ_μ" and the propagator is exponential with the same timescale. So (B) holds in the cum-dividend sense (price plus dividends is the martingale, price alone mean-reverts), and (E) holds. On (N), our reading, not stated in the paper: a trader who does not receive the dividend stream faces a predictable relaxation of the ex-dividend price after any displacement, so an AMM that copied the propagator's price without the cash-flow leg would be pumpable from a displaced state; in the Kyle setting the mean reversion is compensated by the expected dividends over the holding period, and the noise traders' persistence is what keeps the *cum-dividend* price diffusive (the Bouchaud et al. 2004 "critical" balance). This is the same lesson as Brunnermeier–Pedersen from the other side: decay of impact that is *information* decay is safe only if the counterparty is paid the thing whose forecast decays.

#### Prediction-market equilibria on cost-function makers.
Chen, Y., Dimitrov, S., Sami, R., Reeves, D. M., Pennock, D. M., Hanson, R. D., Fortnow, L. and Gonen, R. (2010). "Gaming Prediction Markets: Equilibrium Strategies with a Market Maker." *Algorithmica* 58(4), 930–969. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00453-009-9323-2 (author copy https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/bluffing.pdf). LMSR maker, risk-neutral strategic traders with private signals: with conditionally independent signals, truthful (myopic) betting is the unique weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium in finite and infinite horizons; with unconditionally independent signals traders bluff and there is no finite-time fully revealing equilibrium, a discounted scoring rule restores convergence. Ostrovsky, M. (2012). "Information Aggregation in Dynamic Markets With Strategic Traders." *Econometrica* 80(6), 2595–2647. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA8479 (author copy https://web.stanford.edu/~ost/papers/aggregation3.pdf): for "separable" securities, prices converge to the fully-revealing price in every equilibrium, in both market-scoring-rule markets and Kyle-type dealer markets. Hanson, R. and Oprea, R. (2009). "A Manipulator Can Aid Prediction Market Accuracy." *Economica* 76(302), 304–314. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.2008.00734.x (preprint https://hanson.gmu.edu/biashelp.pdf): a Kyle model with a manipulator who has a quadratic preference for a target price; when the target is uncertain "the mean target price has no effect on prices" and its variance can raise price accuracy by paying informed traders. Verdict: (E) yes in all three; (N) by construction for the LMSR cases (a convex potential, Abernethy et al. above) and by posterior-mean pricing in the Kyle cases; (B) only in the Kyle cases. No clock-time state in any of them. Relation to us: the closest existing E+N results on a *convex potential* maker, with the caveat that the equilibrium analysis concerns information aggregation, not the manipulator's round-trip profit, which is zero by the potential. Verified (abstracts and author copies' abstracts).

### 7.3 Thematic search: "Bayesian market maker + strategic uninformed trader ⇒ no profitable manipulation"

Verdict: no paper states this as a named theorem for a general dynamic model. The closest published statements, with their assumptions, are:

1. Goldstein–Guembel 2008, Proposition 1 (7.2): in *any* equilibrium of the two-round Kyle-type game without feedback, the uninformed speculator never trades in round 1. Assumptions used in the proof: batch prices equal to E[V | order flow] after each round (fill-inclusive in the batch sense), a risk-neutral zero-profit maker, the informed types trading with positive probability in the direction of their information (so a buy raises and a sell lowers the price: symmetric monotone response), and the manipulator's type inside the maker's model. What it does *not* give: zero expected profit for strategies that condition on the speculator's private knowledge of his own past flow (their round-2 remark), or with a payoff outside the ledger (feedback).
2. Kyle 1985's constant-depth argument as restated by Kyle–Viswanathan 2008 (7.2): a non-constant depth profile would let a trader profit "by trading very aggressively", inconsistent with equilibrium. This is "E ⇒ no intertemporal depth arbitrage" and is the only statement found that ties (E) to the *shape of the liquidity profile* rather than to the martingale property of prices; the reduced-form counterparts are Roch–Soner's Λ²/m condition and Fruth et al.'s zero-spread results.
3. Bank–Kramkov II, Lemma 4.8 (7.2): in the indifference-price equilibrium, every strategy with V_T ≥ 0 has V_T = 0, no admissibility needed. Assumptions: strictly concave maker utilities, Pareto reallocation after each trade, no information channel, impact a function of the current position and the exogenous filtration only.
4. Jarrow 1992 and Bank–Baum 2004, Theorem 2.3 (7.1): no arbitrage for small traders when the large trader is inactive, plus a price that depends on the large trader's *current* position only and monotonically, ⇒ no large-trader arbitrage. This is "N from a martingale condition plus a potential", not from (E).
5. Allen–Gorton 1992 as read by Chakraborty–Yılmaz (7.2): a GM specialist whose quotes include the manipulator's strategy leaves the uninformed manipulator with zero expected profit "in every equilibrium", even though the same round trip is profitable against a specialist who ignores him. This is the "policy consistency" ingredient of the brief stated as an equilibrium result, in expectation, for unit trades; the text proving it was not read.
6. Chakraborty–Yılmaz 2004 (7.2): with posterior-mean prices and a best-responding insider, the insider's manipulation is a priced mixed strategy; no uninformed player is modelled, but the reduced-form argument (Jarrow's symmetric unwind) applies to any uninformed loop.

Published failures, each isolating one dropped assumption (this is the counterexample list deliverable K asks for): Allen–Gorton 1992 with a specialist who does not price the manipulator (B holds but the manipulator's sells are treated as liquidity sales: policy consistency dropped); Goldstein–Guembel's round-2 trade (own-history knowledge outside the maker's filtration); Goldstein–Guembel Propositions 2–3, Kumar–Seppi 1992, Fishman–Hagerty 1995 (a payoff linked to the price but outside the maker's ledger); Brunnermeier–Pedersen 2005 (liquidity suppliers who do not forecast predictable flow, i.e. B dropped); Allen–Gale 1992 (risk-averse pooling counterparties and an informed type with a fixed strategy instead of a zero-profit maker facing a best responder); Fruth–Schöneborn–Urusov 2014 zero-spread and Roch–Soner 2013 (depth varying with the clock rather than with the state, i.e. E dropped and the reduced form left free). No paper was found in which (B) holds with full support, the manipulator's schedule is measurable in the maker's filtration, and a sure-profit loop exists; that combination is what the brief's "known so far" paragraph says is impossible, and the literature agrees by having no counterexample rather than by a theorem.

### 7.4 Thematic search: relaxation toward a fraction of the position ("Choice B")

Verdict: the *linear* version of Choice B is the standard block-book model with permanent impact, and nobody has the nonlinear version. In Obizhaeva–Wang 2013 (Section 1 above; NBER WP https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11444/w11444.pdf) the steady state after a trade x₀ is F₀ + s/2 + λx₀, the immediate ask is x₀/q above the old one, and "the difference between the immediate ask and the steady state is x₀(1/q − λ)" which decays exponentially at ρ: the displacement relaxes to λ × (cumulative position), a fraction λq ∈ (0, 1) of its peak. Alfonsi–Fruth–Schied 2010, Section 6 (arXiv:0708.1756), write this explicitly for the block book: D_t = λ Σ ξ_k + Σ κ e^{−ρ(t − t_k)} ξ_k with κ = 1/q − λ, and show the optimal schedule does not depend on λ because the permanent part contributes the fixed potential (λ/2)X₀². Alfonsi–Schied–Slynko 2012 and Gatheral–Schied–Slynko 2012 (Section 1 above) keep a linear permanent part beside a general kernel, and Gatheral 2010's "permanent impact must be linear" result is the reason all of these keep it linear. Roch–Soner 2013 mention the extension "so that the process S converges to a secondary process S⁰ which includes permanent impacts proportional to ℓ_t" and drop it. On the empirical side the target is Farmer et al. 2013's "permanent impact relaxes on average to about two thirds of peak impact" and the propagator literature's G(∞) > 0 (Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart 2004, *Quantitative Finance* 4(2), 176–190, https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680400000022, abstract only). Guéant 2013 (Section 1 above) is the only paper allowing the permanent part to be a *nonlinear* potential of the cumulated position, and Alfonsi–Schied 2010 the only one allowing the transient part to be nonlinear; no paper combines a nonlinear transient with a relaxation target that is a function of the position, and none derives such a target from an equilibrium. The nearest equilibrium relatives are Vodret et al. 2021 (a linear propagator whose decay is information decay, no permanent part because dividends are mean-reverting) and Kyle–Obizhaeva–Wang 2018 (linear permanent in inventory, transient in the rate, no relaxation target). Choice B's safety certificate (G-storage.md Theorem 5) therefore has no literature counterpart to cite; its linear limit is Obizhaeva–Wang with λ = α/q, which is safe by Alfonsi–Schied–Slynko's positive-definiteness plus the linear potential.

### 7.6 Fifteen-line summary for the operator

1. The literature has two separate theorems, not one: reduced-form models prove (N) from "no memory + a martingale condition" (Jarrow 1992; Bank–Baum 2004 Thm 2.3; Çetin–Jarrow–Protter 2004 Thms 3.1–3.2), and equilibrium models prove (N) for uninformed round trips from posterior-mean pricing (Goldstein–Guembel 2008 Prop 1; Chakraborty–Yılmaz 2004; Back–Baruch 2004).
2. No paper proves "equilibrium ⇒ non-manipulable" for a pricing rule with a decaying state; the only equilibrium-with-transient papers (Kyle–Obizhaeva–Wang 2018, Vodret et al. 2021) have no book-resilience state at all: KOW's exponential is the deviator's own inventory running off, Vodret's is a mean-reverting dividend forecast.
3. Bank–Kramkov 2015 is the one model with (E) and (N) together for a nonlinear next-unit price with unbounded positions (II Lemma 4.8, no admissibility needed); its price is a conditional expectation under an inventory-dependent measure, so (B) holds in a risk-sharing sense but not as a posterior on information, and it has no memory.
4. Goldstein–Guembel Prop 1 is the closest theorem of the form the brief wants: any equilibrium, Bayesian batch prices, manipulator as a player ⇒ the uninformed never opens a position; its proof uses only fill-inclusive posterior prices, a zero-profit maker and monotone symmetric response.
5. The same paper shows the gap: an uninformed trader who knows his own past (non-)trades has positive expected profit at round 2, because that knowledge is not in the maker's filtration. This is F-attack.md's "policy consistency" failure in print.
6. Allen–Gorton 1992 is the published test of "policy consistency": with asymmetric liquidity flow, buy-then-sell earns against a specialist who prices sells as liquidity sales, and earns zero "in every equilibrium" once the specialist's quotes include the manipulator (Chakraborty–Yılmaz's reading of the model; the article text itself not read). Symmetric response to a run (odd T on a remembered state) is the AMM version of that quote.
7. Every published uninformed-manipulation profit drops a named assumption: policy consistency (Allen–Gorton's naive specialist), own-history knowledge outside the maker's filtration (Goldstein–Guembel round 2), off-ledger payoff (Kumar–Seppi 1992, Fishman–Hagerty 1995, Goldstein–Guembel with feedback), non-Bayesian liquidity supply (Brunnermeier–Pedersen 2005), risk-averse pooling counterparties with a fixed informed strategy (Allen–Gale 1992), clock-varying depth (Fruth–Schöneborn–Urusov 2014, Roch–Soner 2013).
8. Kyle–Viswanathan 2008's restatement of Kyle 1985 ("market depth must be constant" or a profitable deviation exists) is the one-line "E ⇒ no intertemporal depth arbitrage"; the reduced-form condition it corresponds to is Roch–Soner's Λ²/m supermartingale, i.e. depth may not rise faster than the transient decays squared.
9. For deliverable J the fundamental theorem's shape is fixed by Bank–Baum and Çetin–Jarrow–Protter: without memory, (N) ⟺ the zero-trade price is a martingale under some equivalent measure and the next-unit price is monotone (a convex potential); with memory the potential must be extended to the state, which is M4-SAFETY's storage function, and the literature's only extensions are linear (Roch–Soner, Alfonsi–Schied–Slynko) or nonlinear-without-permanent (Alfonsi–Schied 2010).
10. Abernethy–Chen–Vaughan 2013 prove the converse direction for memoryless makers: path independence forces a cost function and no-arbitrage forces it convex, so "non-pumpable on state P" ⟺ "convex potential" is a theorem there; the analogue with a wait operator is open.
11. Klöck–Schied–Sun 2017 Thm 4.1 is the two-venue result: a second venue trading the same asset must carry full permanent impact and charge at least the permanent potential of its fills, or a horizon exists at which the pair is pumpable. Any oracle or settlement use of the AMM's price is that second venue.
12. Choice B's relaxation toward αP is, in its linear limit, Obizhaeva–Wang's λ (Alfonsi–Fruth–Schied 2010 §6: D = λΣξ + κΣe^{−ρ(t−t_k)}ξ), safe by the linear potential plus a positive-definite kernel; no paper has the nonlinear version or derives the target from a game.
13. Fruth–Schöneborn–Urusov Prop 3.4 gives the cheapest modification for deliverable L: a spread that widens with one's own flow removes standard and transaction-triggered manipulation for any liquidity path, at the cost of wait regions in execution.
14. The papers closest to a joint E+B+N result are Goldstein–Guembel 2008 (E+B, N for round trips, finite horizon, unit trades, no memory), Bank–Kramkov 2015 (E+N, B in the risk-sharing sense, unbounded, no memory), and Back–Baruch 2004 / Chakraborty–Yılmaz 2004 (E+B+N for uninformed loops with a belief state that is the whole history, saturating). None has a decaying state.
15. Theorems found of the form "equilibrium ⇒ non-manipulable", with assumptions: Goldstein–Guembel 2008 Proposition 1 (uninformed round trip loses in every equilibrium; needs posterior-mean batch prices, zero-profit maker, informed types trading with their information with positive probability, no feedback, and does not cover own-history strategies); Bank–Kramkov II Lemma 4.8 (V_T ≥ 0 ⇒ V_T = 0; needs indifference pricing by strictly risk-averse makers with Pareto reallocation and no information channel); Allen–Gorton 1992 in Chakraborty–Yılmaz's reading (zero expected manipulator profit in every equilibrium of a GM market whose specialist prices the manipulator; unit trades, in expectation only). Everything else is a reduced-form no-arbitrage theorem (Jarrow 1992; Bank–Baum 2004; Çetin–Jarrow–Protter 2004; Roch–Soner 2013) whose "equilibrium" is an assumption on the price process, not a game.
